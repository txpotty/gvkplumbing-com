    How to Protect Your Pipes Before the Next New Mexico Freeze

    By Andrew Blake · October 6, 2026 · 5 min read

    Even in milder parts of the state, clear winter nights can drop below freezing, so outdoor spigots and evaporative cooler lines need draining each fall.

    Pipes do not need a long winter to burst. Even mild-weather towns get the occasional night that sits in the low twenties from midnight to sunrise. The houses that get hurt are the ones built as if it never freezes. Supply lines run in exterior walls, the backflow preventer sits bare in the yard, and the pool pump has no cover at all. One night like that can split a pipe that has been fine for twenty years.

    Why Frozen Pipes Burst

    Water expands about nine percent as it freezes. The ice plug itself is rarely what splits the pipe. When the plug forms, it shoves the water ahead of it toward a closed faucet or valve, and pressure builds in that trapped section until the copper splits or a brass valve body cracks. The split usually stays sealed while the line is frozen. So the leak shows up the next morning, when the sun hits the pipe and water starts spraying.

    Above-ground pipe is the weak spot in most houses. Bare copper and PVC lose heat fast on a clear night once the sun goes down. Wind across an open yard pulls heat away even faster. A backflow preventer sitting in the open on a windy night can freeze solid at temperatures that a pipe inside a wall would never notice.

    The Parts of a House Most at Risk

    Before the forecast drops, walk the outside of the house with a flashlight and check these spots:

    • Backflow preventers on irrigation and fire lines. The brass body sits above ground and is not cheap to replace once it cracks.
    • Hose bibs on exterior walls, especially older models that are not frost-free.
    • Pool and spa equipment, meaning the pump, filter, heater and the PVC between them.
    • Irrigation risers and drip manifolds sitting exposed in the beds.
    • Evaporative cooler supply lines that climb the outside wall to the roof, on houses that still run one.
    • Water heaters and supply pipe in unheated garages, mostly in colder areas.
    • The main supply where it comes up out of the ground near the meter or pressure regulator.

    Cheap Steps That Prevent Damage

    Most freeze protection costs less than a service call. Foam sleeves from any hardware store go over the exposed pipe, and a wrap of tape on each seam keeps the cold out. Pull the hose off each hose bib and fit an insulated cover, since a hose left attached holds water right at the valve seat. For a backflow preventer, use an insulated pouch, or wrap it in old towels and set a bucket over it. Where hard freezes show up every winter, a run of outdoor-rated heat tape on the worst stretch pays for itself the first night.

    Check the pool controller for a freeze protection setting. On most newer controllers it kicks the pump on once the air sensor reads a few degrees above freezing. During a hard freeze, let a faucet on an outside wall drip overnight, which relieves pressure and keeps water moving. Open the sink cabinets on exterior walls. The point is to let warm room air reach the supply lines behind the cabinet back.

    High elevation means large daily temperature swings, and that constant expansion and contraction stresses pipe joints and fittings.

    Colder climates call for permanent fixes. A frost-free sillcock moves the shutoff 6 to 12 inches inside the house, where the wall stays warm. Insulated crawlspaces and heat tape on the runs that froze last year handle most of what is left. If you leave a cabin or second home empty for the winter, shut off the main, open the low drains and blow the lines out, because a dry pipe has nothing in it to freeze.

    If a Pipe Freezes

    A trickle from the kitchen faucet on a cold morning usually means a frozen line. Leave that faucet open. Warm the suspect section slowly with a hair dryer or a space heater kept well back. Never use a torch or any open flame. Know exactly where the main shutoff is before you start, since a split pipe will start leaking as soon as it thaws. A split hose bib or one section of pipe usually runs $200 to $500 to repair, and replacing a cracked backflow preventer runs roughly $400 to $900 installed, more for larger sizes.

    When to Call a Professional

    Some freeze damage needs a licensed plumber right away. That means a split pipe or a cracked backflow body, water coming through a ceiling, or a frozen line you cannot reach safely.
