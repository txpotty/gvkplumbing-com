    Slab Leak Warning Signs Every New Mexico Homeowner Should Know

    By Aaron Mitchell · October 15, 2026 · 5 min read

    What a Slab Leak Actually Is

    A lot of houses built since the 1960s sit on a poured concrete slab, with copper or PEX supply lines and cast iron or PVC drains running through or under the concrete. There is nothing wrong with building a house that way. A slab leak starts when one of those buried lines wears a pinhole, splits at a fitting or cracks along its length, and water starts moving into the concrete or the soil below it. Some leak a cup a day, others leak gallons an hour.

    Most homes sit on concrete slabs, so leaks under the floor often call for slab leak detection before any repair begins.

    Soil under any slab swells when it gets wet and shrinks when it dries, and that cycle shifts the concrete by fractions of an inch over the years. Buried pipe handles that movement poorly. Copper rubs against rebar or gravel, joints get pulled, and a line that was fine for twenty years finally lets go.

    Warning Signs Worth Taking Seriously

    A Water Bill That Jumps for No Reason

    Pull up the last six months of bills and compare gallons, not dollars. If usage climbed and your habits did not, something is leaking. A pinhole in a 3/4-inch copper line under normal house pressure can push out several hundred gallons a day, and the meter will show it long before you see a drop anywhere in the house. Shut off every fixture and watch the little leak dial on the meter.

    Warm Spots on the Floor

    Walk the house barefoot. When a hot line leaks under the slab, the concrete above it heats up, and you will feel a patch of tile or laminate that stays warm while the rest of the floor is cool. Dogs and cats often find the warm spot before you do. We confirm it with a thermal camera.

    Running Water With Every Tap Closed

    Late at night, the house finally goes quiet. Kneel in a hallway or closet and listen close to the floor. A hiss or faint rushing sound with every faucet, toilet and appliance shut off usually means pressurized water is escaping somewhere below the slab. A toilet that cycles on its own is a different issue, usually a worn flapper.

    Pressure That Drops Off

    Weak flow at one faucet is usually a clogged aerator. Low pressure at every fixture points somewhere else. When a supply line under the slab is broken, part of the water headed for your shower is going into the dirt instead, so pressure sags across the whole house and gets worse when two fixtures run at once. A screw-on gauge at the hose bib tells us the static pressure in seconds.

    New Cracks in Walls or Floors

    Water moving under a slab washes out fine soil and softens clay, and over a few months the concrete above it can settle unevenly. You start seeing cracked tile grout, drywall cracks over door frames and baseboards pulling away from the wall. Doors that used to close fine start sticking. By then you have a foundation repair on top of the plumbing repair.

    Mold, Mildew and Musty Carpet

    A musty smell in one room, or carpet pad that stays damp along an interior wall, is a common sign that a slab leak has been wicking moisture up through the concrete for a while. Mildew tends to appear low on the drywall and behind the trim. Bleaching it does not fix anything.

    Soggy Spots Outside the Foundation

    Some leaks push water sideways until it surfaces. Look for a strip of grass along the foundation that stays green in a dry spell. Puddles that never dry, soil pulling away from the footing, or a flower bed that is always mud can all trace back to a line under the house.

    When to Call a Professional

    One warning sign by itself can be a coincidence. Two or more together usually are not. Before you reach GVK Plumbing at (505) 581-4975, shut off every fixture, read the water meter, wait an hour without running anything and read it again. If the numbers moved, the leak is on the pressurized side.

    Why Detection Gear Matters

    Finding a slab leak is a locating job before it is a repair job. Our techs pinpoint the break with acoustic sensors, thermal imaging, isolation pressure tests and an electronic line locator. That precision matters because the repair options, a spot repair through the slab or a reroute through the walls and ceilings, both depend on knowing exactly where the break sits and what kind of pipe it is. Guessing means more broken concrete.

    Rerouting is often the better call on older copper. A copper line that has sprung one pinhole after 40 years usually has more coming. A new PEX run through the walls and ceilings skips the concrete altogether, and most homes are back on water the same day or the next, with a few drywall patches as the only cleanup left behind.
